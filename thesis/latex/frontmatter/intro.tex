\chapter{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\section{Relevance of the topic}\label{relevance-of-the-topic}

Urban green space --- and particularly the urban tree canopy --- is a critical asset for any modern city. Trees moderate microclimate, sequester carbon, mitigate the urban heat-island effect, improve air quality and contribute to the well-being of residents. For Astana, located in a continental steppe climate with extreme winter and summer temperatures, every street tree, park area and protective forest belt counts, and the municipal \emph{Zelenstroy} service is responsible for their inventory and maintenance.

Any urban-forestry decision --- where to plant, where to remove diseased specimens, how to compute green-coverage indicators for a new district --- requires an up-to-date inventory of existing trees. The traditional method is a manual field survey: a specialist physically walks each district and records every tree by hand or with a GPS device. For a city of Astana's size this is intractable; comparable surveys for similar cities take one to three years to complete {[}1{]}, and large portions of the inventory are outdated by the time it is finished.

At the same time, high-resolution satellite and aerial imagery covering Astana is freely or inexpensively available from Google Earth, ESRI World Imagery and commercial vendors, and deep-learning-based object detection and instance segmentation have reached a level of maturity that allows the automatic localisation of individual tree crowns directly from such imagery {[}2, 3, 4{]}.

The relevance of this work therefore stems from two converging factors: the practical need of municipal services for a fast, repeatable inventory, and the technological feasibility of producing one with modern deep learning adapted to the specific context of a Central-Asian city.

\section{Object and subject of research}\label{object-and-subject-of-research}

The \textbf{object of research} is the urban green space of the city of Astana --- namely the population of individual trees observable from above in the visible spectrum. The \textbf{subject} is the set of deep-learning models and software components capable of detecting these trees in satellite imagery, segmenting their crowns, converting pixel coordinates into geographic coordinates and presenting the result through a usable web interface.

\section{Aim and objectives}\label{aim-and-objectives}

The \textbf{aim} of the work is to design, implement and evaluate an end-to-end software system that, given a satellite image of an area of Astana, automatically produces an inventory of the trees present in that area, with per-tree geographic coordinates, crown geometry and a confidence score.

\section{Methods of research}\label{methods-of-research}

The work draws on a combination of theoretical and experimental methods: literature analysis of peer-reviewed publications from \emph{Remote Sensing}, \emph{ISPRS Annals} and conference proceedings of 2019--2025; deep-learning model training and fine-tuning with PyTorch, Ultralytics, the DeepForest library, the SAM 2 foundation model and torchvision Mask R-CNN; dataset engineering with the CVAT annotation tool and a model-in-the-loop pre-labelling workflow; software engineering in Python (FastAPI) and JavaScript (React 18, Leaflet); quantitative evaluation using standard COCO metrics on an independent validation set; and qualitative spot-checking on real Astana scenes --- yards, streets, dense residential blocks, parks --- to characterise model behaviour beyond what aggregate metrics capture.

\section{Scientific novelty}\label{scientific-novelty}

The novelty of the work consists in:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The first published evaluation of state-of-the-art deep-learning tree-detection models on satellite imagery of Astana and, more generally, of any major Central-Asian capital. Section 3.6 reports the first measured magnitude of the geographic-generalisation gap for the region: Box mAP@50 = 0.012 for the public NEON-pretrained DeepForest checkpoint on Astana imagery, three orders of magnitude below the same model's reported off-the-shelf F-score on NAIP USA imagery.
\item
  A hybrid four-model architecture (YOLOv8-seg; Mask R-CNN; DeepForest fine-tuned on local data; DeepForest+SAM 2 zero-shot mask refinement) combined through a Weighted-Box-Fusion ensemble of detector outputs, with all branches quantitatively compared on a single dedicated 14-image validation set (Section 3.3).
\item
  A small but novel annotated dataset of Astana satellite imagery, built iteratively as three batches (v1 / v2 / v3) totalling approximately 100 source images with ≈ 5 500 hand-labelled tree-crown polygons (≈ 8 700 polygon instances after sliding-window tiling), with a documented per-image annotation-cost reduction from ≈ 25 minutes (v1, from scratch) to ≈ 4 minutes (v3, model-in-the-loop pre-labelling). The dataset is reusable for future research in the region.
\item
  A systematic 23-experiment hyperparameter ablation of the YOLO branch along six orthogonal axes (model size, starting weights, optimiser, augmentation, inference resolution, training schedule). The ablation establishes the counter-intuitive empirical result that the medium-size YOLOv8m-seg variant (27 M parameters) outperforms the larger YOLOv8x-seg (71 M) and YOLOv8l-seg (46 M) variants by 15--25 \% relative on this dataset scale, and that fresh COCO pre-training beats continuation from a previously fine-tuned checkpoint for the smaller model --- both findings are at odds with the ``use the largest available model'' default.
\item
  A \textbf{cross-YOLO voting ensemble} that combines four YOLOv8 fine-tunes from four distinct generations (v4, two v3-era checkpoints and the pre-regression v2-finetune) through IoU-clustering and a K-of-N majority vote. Because the ``stadium-roof'' false positive is a v4-generation regression, mixing in earlier-generation members lets the vote suppress it --- demonstrated qualitatively in Section 3.5 (a full quantitative M14 evaluation of the ensembles is left to future work).
\item
  A complete reusable software template --- a FastAPI backend with a pluggable four-model adapter interface, a Leaflet-based frontend with in-browser ESRI and Google tile capture (Auto-Zoom Region Scan and Polygon Scan with streaming progress), SQLite persistent storage of every snapshot and detection, and three exporters (GeoJSON, CSV, standalone HTML) --- that turns research models into a deployable internal tool.
\end{itemize}

\section{Practical significance}\label{practical-significance}

The resulting system is directly applicable to the day-to-day work of Astana's \emph{Zelenstroy} and of any city administration concerned with the management of urban green spaces. The user supplies a satellite image (or selects an area on an interactive map) and receives in seconds a list of detected trees with geographic coordinates, crown geometry, an interactive Leaflet visualisation and standard GIS-compatible exports (GeoJSON for QGIS, CSV for spreadsheet analysis, standalone HTML for sharing). The architecture is designed to be retrained on imagery from any other city by replacing the dataset, with no changes to the pipeline.

\section{Structure of the work}\label{structure-of-the-work}

The diploma project consists of an introduction, three chapters, a conclusion, a list of references and appendices. \textbf{Chapter 1} analyses the subject area, surveys the relevant literature of 2019--2025 in table form, identifies the research gap for Central-Asian cities, and formulates the problem statement. \textbf{Chapter 2} describes the methodology --- the system architecture, the four deep-learning models, the data-preparation pipeline, the ensemble strategy, the geographic-conversion stage, the persistence layer and the web application. \textbf{Chapter 3} documents the experiments and reports quantitative and qualitative results --- the hardware and dataset configuration, the ablation summary, the cross-model comparison on the M14 validation set, the cross-YOLO voting ensemble, the out-of-distribution evaluation against published baselines and the limitations of the current implementation. The conclusion summarises the results, evaluates the achievement of each of the six objectives, and outlines directions for future work.
